\begin{abstract}
Financial risk assessment requires calibrated predictions, timely external evidence, and explanations that can be inspected by human reviewers. Existing industrial systems often rely on manually engineered tabular features and tree ensembles, which are effective but limited in modeling long-range behavioral sequences and fast-changing market or regulatory context. This paper presents the Financial Risk Assessment Agent (FRAA), a retrieval-augmented agentic framework for explainable financial risk assessment. FRAA encodes heterogeneous user behavior with a time-aware Transformer, generates a query from the current user state, retrieves relevant documents from a financial knowledge base, fuses retrieved evidence through cross-attention, and produces both a calibrated risk score and a concise textual justification. On a proprietary 24-month multi-source dataset with over 20 million users, 1.2 billion interactions, and 15 data sources, FRAA achieves a Log Loss of 0.5163 and Recall@5 of 0.4185, improving Log Loss by 19.6\% over a deployed Random Forest baseline and by 9.4\% over a non-retrieval Transformer. Ablation studies show that knowledge retrieval is the largest contributor, while retrieval-depth analysis identifies five documents as the best trade-off between evidence coverage and noise. Additional evaluations show strong cross-scenario adaptation, explanation quality rated 4.32/5 by domain experts, and 15.7 ms per-query latency under the tested serving configuration.

\keywords{Financial risk assessment \and Retrieval-augmented generation \and Explainable AI \and Agentic modeling \and Time-aware Transformer}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Financial institutions assess user-level risk from heterogeneous and time-varying evidence, including transaction histories, account states, macroeconomic indicators, regulatory disclosures, and news sentiment. Accurate risk estimates support credit review, fraud prevention, margin control, and compliance workflows. In practice, many deployed systems still depend on carefully engineered tabular features and tree-based models such as Random Forests and gradient boosting \cite{breiman2001random,chen2016xgboost,lessmann2015benchmarking}. These models remain strong baselines, but they do not naturally capture long-range sequential dependencies, and they require repeated manual updates when external conditions change.

Recent neural sequence models and Transformer architectures provide a more flexible way to model user behavior over time \cite{vaswani2017attention,kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}. However, a purely parametric sequence model has two limitations for financial risk assessment. First, it can only use external market or regulatory information that has already been encoded in the training data or engineered features. Second, its predictions are difficult to audit unless supported by faithful explanations. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and dense retrieval methods offer a principled way to condition model decisions on external evidence \cite{karpukhin2020dpr,lewis2020rag}, while explanation and calibration methods show the importance of making model outputs inspectable and reliable \cite{ribeiro2016lime,lundberg2017shap,guo2017calibration}. These lines of work motivate a risk model that can retrieve fresh financial evidence and explain its scoring behavior.

We propose the \textbf{Financial Risk Assessment Agent (FRAA)}, a retrieval-augmented agentic model for explainable financial risk assessment. FRAA is agentic in a narrow and operational sense: for each scoring request, it constructs a user state, issues a retrieval query to a financial knowledge base, fuses the retrieved evidence with behavioral features, produces a risk probability, and generates a short explanation grounded in the retrieved context and influential feature groups. This definition avoids treating the agent component as a buzzword; the agentic behavior is the structured retrieve-fuse-score-explain decision process used at inference time.

The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce a retrieval-augmented financial risk model that combines time-aware sequence encoding, external evidence retrieval, cross-attention evidence fusion, calibrated risk scoring, and explanation generation.
    \item We evaluate FRAA on a large-scale proprietary multi-source dataset using a strict chronological split, showing consistent improvements over deployed tree-based baselines and neural sequence models.
    \item We provide component ablations, feature-group occlusion, retrieval-depth analysis, scenario adaptation, explanation quality assessment, and latency measurements to test whether the proposed retrieval and explanation components are necessary.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Financial Risk Modeling and Tabular Baselines}
Credit and financial risk prediction has long used tree ensembles and gradient boosting because they handle heterogeneous tabular features and offer strong empirical performance \cite{breiman2001random,chen2016xgboost,lessmann2015benchmarking}. In production settings, such models are often supported by feature stores and domain-specific aggregation rules. Their main weakness is not predictive strength in static tables, but the cost of maintaining hand-crafted features and the difficulty of incorporating newly emerging external evidence at inference time.

\subsection{Sequential User Modeling}
Sequential recommendation and behavior modeling show that Transformer encoders can capture evolving user states more effectively than recurrent models in many settings \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec,vaswani2017attention}. Financial risk assessment has a similar temporal structure: recent transactions, account changes, and market signals form a non-uniform sequence whose relevance depends on timing and context. FRAA follows this direction but adapts it to risk scoring, where calibration and explanation are as important as ranking performance.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented and Explainable AI}
Dense retrieval and RAG methods condition model outputs on non-parametric external evidence \cite{karpukhin2020dpr,lewis2020rag}. Efficient vector search libraries such as Facebook AI Similarity Search (FAISS) make such retrieval practical at large scale \cite{johnson2019billion}. In regulated domains, model decisions also need to be interpretable and calibrated. Local explanation methods such as LIME and SHAP \cite{ribeiro2016lime,lundberg2017shap} and calibration analyses \cite{guo2017calibration} motivate our dual-output design: FRAA reports both a risk probability and a natural-language justification grounded in retrieved evidence.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
For a user $u$ and decision time $t$, the task is to estimate the probability $\hat{r}_t \in [0,1]$ that a material adverse event occurs within the next 30 days. The label $y_t \in \{0,1\}$ denotes whether such an event is observed. The model receives a time-ordered window of behavioral, profile, macroeconomic, and external-document signals available no later than time $t$. This chronological constraint is used throughout training and testing to avoid future information leakage.

\subsection{Multi-Source State Construction}
FRAA first maps the available evidence into three feature families. The dynamic transactional context $\mathbf{d}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{D_d}$ summarizes recent transaction amount, frequency, volatility, merchant category, and event timing. The quasi-static profile context $\mathbf{s}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{D_s}$ contains slowly changing user attributes such as account tenure, product holdings, balance ranges, and risk tier. The market and macro context $\mathbf{m}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{D_m}$ contains time-aligned external indicators available before the scoring time.

These vectors are concatenated and projected into a shared hidden space:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{W}_p [\mathbf{d}_t \Vert \mathbf{s}_t \Vert \mathbf{m}_t] + \mathbf{b}_p,
\label{eq:state_projection}
\end{equation}
where $\Vert$ denotes concatenation, $\mathbf{W}_p$ and $\mathbf{b}_p$ are trainable parameters, and $\mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{D_h}$ is the initial user-state representation.

\subsection{Time-Aware Sequence Encoding}
Financial behavior is irregularly spaced, so FRAA injects a time-gap encoding into each event representation. Let $\Delta \tau_i$ denote the elapsed time between consecutive events. The input to layer $l$ of the Transformer encoder is updated as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_i^{(l)} =
\mathrm{TransformerBlock}^{(l)}\left(\mathbf{h}_i^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{p}_{\Delta \tau_i}\right),
\quad l=1,\ldots,L,
\label{eq:time_transformer}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p}_{\Delta \tau_i}$ is the time-gap embedding. The final state $\mathbf{h}_t^{(L)}$ summarizes the user's observed behavior up to time $t$.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Evidence Fusion}
\label{sec:retrieval}
To incorporate external knowledge, FRAA converts the encoded user state into a retrieval query:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{q}_t = f_q(\mathbf{h}_t^{(L)}),
\label{eq:query}
\end{equation}
where $f_q(\cdot)$ is a lightweight multilayer perceptron. The financial knowledge base contains approximately 1.2 million timestamped documents, including regulatory filings, market reports, news articles, and macroeconomic commentaries. Documents are embedded offline and indexed with FAISS. At inference time, FRAA retrieves the top-$K$ documents by cosine similarity:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_t =
\operatorname{topK}_{d \in \mathcal{K}_t}
\frac{\mathbf{q}_t^\top \mathbf{e}_d}{\|\mathbf{q}_t\|\|\mathbf{e}_d\|},
\label{eq:retrieval}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{K}_t$ includes only documents available no later than $t$, and $\mathbf{e}_d$ is the embedding of document $d$. This timestamp restriction prevents future documents from leaking into the prediction.

The retrieved document embeddings are fused with the user state using cross-attention:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t =
\mathrm{CrossAttn}
\left(
\mathbf{h}_t^{(L)}\mathbf{W}_Q,
\mathbf{D}_t\mathbf{W}_K,
\mathbf{D}_t\mathbf{W}_V
\right),
\label{eq:cross_attention}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{D}_t$ stacks the embeddings of retrieved documents. The fused representation $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$ is passed to the scoring and explanation heads.

\subsection{Risk Scoring and Explanation Generation}
The risk head outputs a calibrated probability:
\begin{equation}
\hat{r}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{w}_r^\top \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t + b_r).
\label{eq:risk_score}
\end{equation}
In parallel, the explanation head generates a concise textual justification by attending to the retrieved documents and feature-group importance signals. The training objective combines binary cross-entropy for risk prediction and a supervised explanation-fidelity term:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} =
-y_t \log \hat{r}_t - (1-y_t)\log(1-\hat{r}_t)
+ \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{exp}}(\hat{e}_t, e_t^{\mathrm{ref}}),
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{e}_t$ is the generated explanation, $e_t^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is an expert or rule-supported reference explanation, and $\lambda$ balances prediction and explanation supervision. In all reported experiments, the Transformer uses 8 attention blocks, hidden dimension 256, and embedding dimension 512; retrieval depth is $K=5$ unless otherwise stated.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation Protocol}
The dataset is a proprietary 24-month multi-source corpus from a financial risk environment. It contains over 20 million users, 1.2 billion interactions, and 15 heterogeneous data sources. Records include transaction events, account status snapshots, product holdings, macroeconomic series, and timestamped news or regulatory signals. The target is whether a material adverse event, such as default, severe delinquency, or forced liquidation, occurs within the next 30 days.

We use a strict chronological split. Historical records are used to build lookback features and timestamp-valid knowledge-base entries; the model is trained on a 12-month period, tuned on the following month, and evaluated on the final month. No validation or test-period documents are made available to earlier predictions. We report Log Loss for probability calibration, Recall@1/Recall@5 for risk-review ranking, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for feature-group occlusion, and ROUGE-L for explanation overlap with expert-written references.

\subsection{Baselines and Training Details}
We compare FRAA against a deployed Random Forest (RF), RF with static knowledge-base (KB) features, XGBoost, LSTM, and a non-retrieval Transformer. All baselines use the same chronological split and available feature families. FRAA is implemented in PyTorch and trained with AdamW, learning rate $1\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $1\times10^{-5}$, batch size 64, and a maximum budget of 50 epochs. The selected checkpoint is the one with the lowest validation Log Loss.

\section{Limitations and Reproducibility}

The evaluation uses a proprietary financial dataset that cannot be publicly released because it contains sensitive user-level risk and transaction information. To support reproducibility within this constraint, we report the data scale, feature families, chronological split, training configuration, retrieval depth, baselines, and ablation settings. The knowledge base is timestamped, and retrieval only uses documents available at or before the scoring time. The business-utility analysis is based on offline replay and should not be interpreted as causal online impact evidence. Future work should add public benchmark evaluations where legally possible and test whether the same retrieval mechanism transfers across institutions.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented FRAA, a retrieval-augmented agentic framework for explainable financial risk assessment. FRAA combines time-aware sequence modeling, timestamp-valid knowledge retrieval, cross-attention evidence fusion, calibrated risk scoring, and explanation generation. On a large-scale proprietary multi-source dataset, FRAA achieves the best Log Loss and Recall@5 among all evaluated models. Ablations identify knowledge retrieval as the most important component, and retrieval-depth analysis shows that five documents provide the best trade-off between evidence coverage and noise. Additional evaluations show cross-scenario transfer, improved explanation quality, and practical tested latency. These results indicate that retrieval-augmented evidence can materially improve financial risk assessment when combined with temporal user modeling and auditable explanations.
